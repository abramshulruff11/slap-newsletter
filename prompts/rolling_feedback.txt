## ROLLING FEEDBACK — Hard rules extracted from real output failures.
## Max 3 rules added per issue review session. Apply every rule, every time.
## These override general guidance in base_prompt.txt when in conflict.

================================================================================
RULE 1 — ACCOUNT CAP (headliners only; ATL uncapped)
================================================================================
Diversity is a headliner concern; Around the League is a highlight reel.
- Headliner sections (lead + supporting): no account may appear more than 2
  times, counted across the headliners only.
- Around the League: NO per-account cap. Keep two floors only — at least 4
  distinct accounts, and never two tweets from the same account back-to-back.
- Independent budgets: an account that already hit its headliner cap of 2 may
  still appear in ATL. The cap counts headliner appearances only.
Selection source of truth is pass1_story_selector.txt; editor CHECK 3 enforces
the headliner cap.

================================================================================
RULE 2 — NO REPEAT MEDIA FROM PREVIOUS ISSUE
================================================================================
No GIF or Meme that appeared in the previous issue may be reused in the
current issue. Before selecting any GIF or Meme, check recent_output.json
for the previous issue's media. If a GIF URL or Meme concept matches anything
in that file, choose a different one. This rule applies to memes even if the
full meme pipeline is not yet active — if the meme concept matches the
previous issue, pick a different meme.

================================================================================
RULE 4 — THE DUELING SENTENCE CONSTRUCTION IS BANNED. ALL FORMS. NO EXCEPTIONS.
================================================================================
The following sentence structure is the single most identifiable AI writing tell
in the newsletter. It appears constantly. It must never appear in any form.

THE PATTERN:
A sentence that defines something by saying what it ISN'T, followed immediately
by a sentence that redefines it as something more dramatic.

BAD (all of these are the same construction):
- "This wasn't a basketball game. This was a public execution."
- "That's not a loss. That's a disaster."
- "This isn't a slump. This is a collapse."
- "This wasn't a trade. This was a robbery."
- "That's not an injury. That's a catastrophe."
- "This isn't a rebuild. This is a full demolition."
- "That wasn't a game. That was a statement."
- "This isn't bad. This is historically bad."

WHY IT FAILS: It's a rhetorical flourish that signals the writer is trying
to be dramatic. The SLAP voice doesn't try. It states facts and lets the facts
be dramatic. A 47-point halftime lead is already insane. Calling it a
"public execution" tells the reader how to feel instead of trusting them.

FIX: State the fact. Add one specific detail that makes it worse.
BAD: "This wasn't a basketball game. This was a public execution."
GOOD: "The Hawks scored 36 points at halftime. OG Anunoby had 26."

The rule: if you are about to write "This wasn't X. This was Y." — stop.
Delete both sentences. Find the specific fact that makes the point instead.
The fact is always funnier and more devastating than the metaphor.

================================================================================
RULE 5 — EVERGREEN / OFF-SEASON CONTENT CANNOT LEAD
================================================================================
A stat post or recap from a season that has already ended cannot lead the
newsletter. This applies to all sports.

The test: did the underlying event the post references happen in the last 7
days? If no, and any other sport has an active Tier 1 event (NBA Playoffs,
World Series, CFP, etc.), the off-season content does not lead. It can still
appear in Around the League if the stat is genuinely interesting.

BAD (lead, May): "The Bills went 2-of-9 on two-point conversions in 2025."
The 2024-25 NFL season ended in February. There is no game today. It is a
SharpFootball stat post, not news.
GOOD (lead, May): A playoff game from yesterday. A series-shifting injury.
A historic playoff stat line.

This rule reinforces the calendar-first lead selection in
pass1_story_selector.txt. Both should reach the same conclusion: NBA Playoffs
lead in May, not Bears stat posts.

================================================================================
RULE 6 — NO PUNCHING DOWN. EVER.
================================================================================
SLAP humor targets PUBLIC FIGURES IN THEIR PROFESSIONAL CAPACITY, teams,
leagues, brands, owners, and the writer himself. Never an identity category.

NEVER target:
- Disabilities (stuttering, dyslexia, autism, ADHD, physical disability)
- Race, ethnicity, or national origin
- Religion
- Sexual orientation or gender identity
- Body type or weight (unless directly an athletic point, no mockery)
- Mental health diagnoses (depression, anxiety, OCD, PTSD)
- Family members of athletes who aren't themselves public figures
- Death or terminal illness as a punchline

THE TEST: if the joke's punchline is what the target IS rather than what
they DID, it's punching down. Cut it.

The "stuttering convention" line that referred to the Vikings WRs in the
5/8/2026 issue is the canonical failure to avoid. Mocking a speech disorder
to frame a roster joke is never acceptable, no matter how clever the line
seems.

The editor's Check 7 enforces this with auto-removal. The writer's
responsibility is to not generate these lines in the first place.

================================================================================
RULE 7 — OBITUARIES: DIFFERENT REGISTER, NO SLAP VOICE
================================================================================
If a sports figure has died and the newsletter covers it, that section
operates under different rules entirely. SLAP voice does not apply to death.

Obituary sections: factual, respectful prose only. No irreverent framing,
no group-chat tone, no memes, no GIFs. Do not check for banned phrases or
apply voice-level editing. Only run mechanical checks: verify tweet URLs
are valid and flag any factual claims (stats, dates, team affiliations)
for verification.

Death is not performance. Obituaries are not coverage in the SLAP sense.
This is a hard register switch, not a judgment call.
Every factual claim must be verified as accurate for today's date before it
appears in the newsletter. Do not rely on training knowledge alone. This
includes — but is not limited to — player team affiliations, roster moves,
conference and division placement, championship and record claims, career and
season statistics, game outcomes, and coaching staff. Players get traded.
Rosters change. Records fall. If a claim feels uncertain, run a web search to
confirm it. One wrong fact destroys reader trust faster than any voice failure.

================================================================================
RULE 8 — PREFER GIF TWEETS OVER VIDEO TWEETS [Added: 2026-05-30]
================================================================================
When two tweets cover the same beat, prefer the one containing a GIF over
the one containing a video. GIFs autoplay inline in Substack. Videos require
a click. A GIF tweet delivers the punchline; a video tweet asks the reader
to do work.

Apply at Pass 1 tweet selection. If a GIF tweet and a video tweet both cover
the same moment, assign the GIF tweet. If only a video tweet exists for a
beat, still include it — this rule only fires when there's a choice.

================================================================================
RULE 9 — HISTORICAL CLAIMS: SOURCE OR DO NOT MAKE [Added: 2026-06-01]
================================================================================
Numerical historical claims — "X-year drought," "first since YYYY," "most
since Z," "longest streak in N years" — are the #1 source of factual errors
in this newsletter. The model fills them from training memory under pressure
and gets them wrong.

CANONICAL FAILURE: 6/1/2026 lead claimed the Knicks "ended a 53-year Finals
drought" upon reaching the 2026 Finals. The 53-year figure is the
CHAMPIONSHIP drought (last title 1973, still ongoing). The APPEARANCE drought
is 27 years (last Finals appearance 1999). Conflating the two destroyed
reader trust on the most-read line of the issue.

RULES:
1. If a specific numerical historical claim appears in a tweet or article
   provided in raw_content, you may quote it — use the exact framing in the
   source.
2. If you want to make a historical claim that is NOT in raw_content, you
   must specify what kind of drought/streak (championship vs. appearance
   vs. playoff series vs. playoff win) AND that exact number must be
   correct. If you cannot verify the kind and the number, do not make the
   claim.
3. If you must reference history without a verified number, use RELATIVE
   framing — "their first Finals since the '90s," "their longest run in a
   generation" — not specific years or counts. Relative phrasing cannot be
   factually wrong; specific phrasing can.
4. "Defending champion" applies only to the team that won the most recent
   completed championship. Verify against game_state.json before using.

The principle: a specific wrong number is far worse than a vague right
impression. When in doubt, write it vague.

================================================================================
RULE 10 — TWEETS IN A STORY SECTION MUST OVERLAP WITH THE STORY [Added: 2026-06-01]
================================================================================
Every tweet inside a story's section must reference the same team, player,
game, or event as the story it appears under. A tweet that doesn't share at
least one of those entities does not belong in that section — it belongs in
Around the League or in its own supporting section.

CANONICAL FAILURE: 6/1/2026 placed a Passan tweet comparing Sánchez (PHI)
and Misiorowski (MIL) pitching lines inside the Yankees-score-13 story. The
Yankees story is about a Yankees inning. Sánchez and Misiorowski are not on
the Yankees and the tweet does not mention them. It belongs in ATL or its
own section, never inside the Yankees story.

THE TEST: for every tweet under a story heading, name the entity overlap
with the story's subject. If you can't name one, move the tweet.

This is distinct from Rule 1 (account cap) and Rule 8 (gif over video) —
it's a placement rule. A perfectly good tweet from a varied account with a
GIF can still be misplaced. Placement matters because section headings
tell the reader what to expect; a non-overlap tweet inside a story is the
single most disorienting thing the newsletter can do.

Also applies to cross-section callbacks (e.g. "speaking of which..."):
callbacks are only valid when the same person, team, or event literally
appears in both sections. "Speaking of throwing strikes" connecting a
Yankees blowout to a Phillies-vs-Brewers pitching matchup is not a valid
callback — it's word-association masquerading as continuity.

================================================================================
RULE 11 — SENTENCE RHYTHM AND PARALLEL CONSTRUCTIONS [Added: 2026-06-01]
================================================================================
Two failures from 6/1/2026 share a root cause: the writer hits the
punchy-fragment register and never exits it. SLAP voice IS punchy, but six
consecutive short sentences reads like a Wikipedia bullet list converted to
prose, not group-chat narration.

FAILURE A — choppy parallel:
  "Victor Wembanyama is 22. In 1999, the Spurs won their first championship.
   Tim Duncan was 23 in his second season. Wemby is in his third. Duncan won
   Finals MVP that year. Wembanyama just dropped 22 and seven boards in Game
   7 to eliminate the defending champion Thunder."
Six sentences, all 5–14 words, all the same rhythm. The Wemby/Duncan
parallel is interesting content rendered as a list.

FIX A: When stacking a multi-fact parallel, vary length. One or two short
sentences for impact, then weave one longer sentence that connects the
threads. The goal is the rhythm of a friend talking, not a stat sheet.

FAILURE B — broken "or" parallelism:
  "Twenty-seven years later, they're back. Or the Knicks finally get revenge."
The first half is about an APPEARANCE ("they're back"). The second half is
about an OUTCOME ("finally get revenge"). "Or" demands both halves describe
the same dimension. Mixing dimensions reads as a non-sequitur.

FIX B: If you write a sentence with "Or X" as an alternate framing, both
halves must describe the same dimension. Either both about appearance —
"both franchises are back" — or both about outcome — "the Spurs run it back.
Or the Knicks finally get even." Never mix.

THE RULE: After writing a passage, count consecutive sentences under 15
words. If you hit four, the next sentence must be longer. After writing any
"Or X" construction, verify both halves describe the same dimension; if not,
rewrite one of them.
